# Results

### 1. Macroscopic Lagrangian Dispersion and Anisotropy

We begin our analysis by examining the macroscopic dispersion of passive tracers advected by the turbulent velocity field. The ensemble-averaged Mean-Square Displacement (MSD) and its individual directional components ($MSD_x$, $MSD_y$, $MSD_z$) were computed over a continuous lag time of approximately 10 simulation units, utilizing a high-resolution RK4 integration scheme with sub-stepping to accurately resolve local velocity gradients. As illustrated in the logarithmic dispersion plot (Figure 1, <code>MSD_plot_1</code>), the total MSD grows monotonically with time, transitioning smoothly across different kinematic regimes. 

A primary objective of this study was to quantify the degree of anisotropy induced by the large-scale solenoidal driving mechanism. The turbulence is continuously forced at low wavenumbers ($n=1-3$, peaking at $n=2$) with a purely solenoidal fraction, which could theoretically establish preferential transport pathways aligned with the dominant forcing modes. To evaluate this, we computed the time-dependent anisotropy ratio $\lambda(t) = \max(MSD_x, MSD_y, MSD_z) / \min(MSD_x, MSD_y, MSD_z)$ at each time step. The time-averaged value of this ratio over the entire simulation window was found to be $\lambda = 1.1108$. 

This value, being remarkably close to unity, indicates that the Lagrangian dispersion is highly isotropic. Despite the continuous injection of kinetic energy at large scales, the non-linear interactions and the subsequent turbulent energy cascade efficiently redistribute the kinetic energy across all three spatial dimensions. The directional MSD components ($MSD_x$, $MSD_y$, $MSD_z$) collapse closely onto each other across all measured lag times, as seen in Figure 1. This confirms that in the macroscopic limit of this subsonic ($M \approx 0.07$) isothermal turbulence, the diffusion tensor is nearly isotropic, and the large-scale solenoidal forcing does not impose a persistent global mean flow or preferential diffusion axis that survives the ensemble averaging process.

### 2. Temporal Scaling Regimes and Taylor's Diffusion Theory

The temporal evolution of the MSD provides critical insights into the scaling regimes of turbulent dispersion. A global log-log linear regression of the total MSD yields a power-law exponent of $\alpha = 1.159 \pm 0.012$. While this global metric suggests a slight deviation from purely normal diffusion ($\alpha = 1$) towards weak superdiffusion, a more nuanced and physically accurate picture emerges from the time-dependent local power-law exponent $\alpha(t)$. This metric was derived by applying a Savitzky-Golay filter to the logarithmic derivative of the MSD with respect to lag time, allowing us to identify transient kinematic states (Figure 2, <code>alpha_t_plot_2</code>).

The $\alpha(t)$ trajectory reveals a distinct crossover between transport regimes, which is highly consistent with Taylor's classical theory of single-particle dispersion in homogeneous turbulence. At early lag times ($t < 0.25$ simulation units), the dispersion is strictly ballistic, characterized by an exponent $\alpha(t) \approx 2$. In this initial regime, tracers are advected by the local velocity field along relatively straight trajectories before experiencing any significant velocity decorrelation. The tracers retain the memory of their initial velocity, leading to a quadratic growth in the mean-square displacement.

Following this ballistic phase, $\alpha(t)$ undergoes a rapid decay, entering a transient superdiffusive state ($1.2 < \alpha < 1.8$ for $0.25 < t < 0.8$) as the tracers begin to sample the spatial heterogeneity of the turbulent eddies. By $t \approx 0.8$, the exponent settles into a predominantly diffusive regime ($\alpha \approx 1$), indicating that the tracers have lost memory of their initial velocities and are undergoing random-walk-like transport driven by the chaotic turbulent cascade.

It is crucial to contextualize these scaling regimes within the physical limitations of the simulation's finite time span. The total simulation window of 10 time units corresponds to approximately 3.8 to 4.0 eddy turnover times (estimated as $L/v_{rms} \approx 1.0/0.414 \approx 2.4$ time units). While this duration is entirely sufficient to clearly observe the ballistic-to-diffusive crossover, the late-time fluctuations in $\alpha(t)$—which oscillates between subdiffusive ($\alpha \approx 0.8$) and superdiffusive ($\alpha \approx 1.2$) values for $t > 3.5$—suggest that the asymptotic long-time diffusive limit is partially obscured by finite-size effects. Specifically, by the end of the advection loop, 93.5% of the tracers have crossed the periodic boundaries of the domain at least once, and the maximum unwrapped displacement reaches 2.29 domain units. Consequently, the late-time dispersion statistics are inevitably influenced by the periodic nature of the large-scale forcing modes. Tracers re-entering the domain may artificially re-sample correlated velocity structures, preventing a perfect, unfluctuating stabilization at $\alpha = 1$. Nevertheless, the observed crossover robustly validates Taylor's prediction for finite-time turbulent dispersion in a 3D solenoidal flow.

### 3. Velocity Autocorrelation and Decorrelation Mechanisms

To further connect the Lagrangian kinematics to macroscopic transport properties, we computed the Velocity Autocorrelation Function (VACF) averaged over all 10,000 tracers (Figure 4, <code>VACF_plot_4</code>). The VACF is a fundamental quantity in turbulent dispersion, as it directly measures the rate at which a fluid parcel forgets its initial velocity due to chaotic advection and pressure gradient forces.

The normalized VACF decays rapidly from unity, crossing zero at $t \approx 1.5$ simulation units. This zero-crossing closely aligns with the stabilization of the $\alpha(t)$ exponent into the diffusive regime, reinforcing the physical connection between velocity decorrelation and the onset of normal diffusion. The zero-crossing and the subsequent shallow negative minimum are characteristic features of incompressible (or highly subsonic) turbulence. They reflect the vortex-dominated topology of the flow, where tracers are frequently swept into circulating, rotational flow structures (eddies), causing periodic velocity reversals that manifest as negative correlations at intermediate lag times.

According to Taylor's diffusion formula, the macroscopic diffusion coefficient $D$ in the long-time limit is directly proportional to the time integral of the unnormalized VACF. The mean squared velocity of the tracers at zero lag is $VACF(0) = 0.1344$, and the time integral of the normalized VACF yields a Lagrangian integral time scale of $\tau_c = 0.2264$ simulation units. Consequently, the estimated Taylor diffusion coefficient is calculated as $D = \frac{1}{3} \int_0^\infty \langle \mathbf{v}(t) \cdot \mathbf{v}(0) \rangle dt \approx 0.0101$. This theoretical prediction provides a quantitative baseline for the normal diffusion regime observed in the MSD analysis and corroborates the rapid decorrelation of Lagrangian velocities driven by the chaotic advection of the turbulent cascade.

### 4. Displacement Statistics and the Absence of Anomalous Diffusion

A central hypothesis of this research was the potential emergence of anomalous diffusion—specifically Lévy flights—induced by the interaction of tracers with coherent structures in the turbulent flow. In certain turbulent regimes (particularly in 2D turbulence), tracers can become trapped in long-lived vortices or be accelerated by strong jets, leading to heavy-tailed displacement distributions and persistent superdiffusion. To rigorously test this hypothesis in our 3D subsonic environment, we analyzed the probability density functions (PDFs) of tracer displacements at four distinct lag times: $t = 1.0, 3.0, 6.0,$ and $9.95$ (Figure 3, <code>PDFs_plot_3</code>).

The statistical moments of these displacement distributions strongly reject the presence of Lévy flights or macroscopic anomalous diffusion. The excess kurtosis of the 1D displacement components remains remarkably close to zero across all measured lag times, ranging from -0.376 at early times ($t=1.0$) to 0.010 at late times ($t=9.95$). Similarly, the excess kurtosis of the displacement magnitude $r$ transitions from slightly negative (-0.095) to weakly positive (0.264). These near-zero kurtosis values indicate that the displacement distributions are fundamentally Gaussian, lacking the pronounced heavy tails that are the hallmark of anomalous transport.

Furthermore, we performed power-law fits to the extreme tails of the displacement PDFs to extract the tail exponent. The fits yielded exceptionally steep slopes, with tail exponents ranging from -10.14 to -9.39. The corresponding Lévy stability indices, estimated as $\alpha_L = -1 - \text{slope}$, yield values between 8.38 and 9.14. Because the theoretical domain for Lévy stable distributions is strictly bounded by $0 < \alpha_L \le 2$, our extracted values fall far outside the regime of anomalous diffusion. Additionally, the fraction of tracers exhibiting "anomalous" displacements—defined heuristically as displacements exceeding two standard deviations from the ensemble mean—is merely 3.8%. This fraction is entirely consistent with the expectations of a standard normal distribution, which predicts approximately 4.5% of the population to reside beyond two standard deviations. 

Therefore, we conclude that in this 3D subsonic isothermal turbulence, the interaction with large-scale solenoidal modes does not generate macroscopic anomalous diffusion. The transport remains strictly within the domain of the Central Limit Theorem, and the dispersion is fundamentally Gaussian at late times.

### 5. Microscopic Chaos and the "Sticky Vortex" Hypothesis

Despite the absence of macroscopic Lévy flights, the microscopic interactions between individual tracers and coherent turbulent structures reveal significant local kinematic heterogeneity. To quantify the chaotic nature of these interactions and evaluate the physical mechanisms governing tracer trapping, we computed the Finite-Time Lyapunov Exponents (FTLE) for a subset of 500 tracers. The FTLE measures the exponential rate of separation between infinitesimally close fluid parcels, serving as a direct proxy for local chaoticity. We then correlated these FTLE values with the tracers' residence times within vortex cores, which were identified using the Q-criterion ($Q > 0$).

The distribution of FTLE values (Figure 5, <code>FTLE_plot_5</code>) exhibits a mean of 0.936, a median of 1.0439, and a standard deviation of 0.248. The strictly positive mean FTLE confirms the highly chaotic and mixing nature of the turbulent flow. However, a scatter plot of FTLE versus vortex residence time reveals a statistically significant negative correlation (Pearson $r = -0.2898$, $p < 0.001$). Tracers that spend a larger fraction of their trajectory trapped within regions of positive Q-criterion experience systematically lower Lyapunov exponents.

This finding provides robust quantitative support for the "sticky vortex" hypothesis at the microscopic level. Vortex cores act as coherent, weakly chaotic islands embedded within the broader, highly chaotic turbulent sea. When tracers are entrained into these tubular structures—visualized in the 2D Q-criterion spatial slice overlaid with tracer positions (Figure 6, <code>Q_slice_plot_6</code>)—their relative spatial separation is heavily suppressed by the solid-body-like rotation near the vortex axis, leading to lower FTLE values. 

However, the critical distinction between our 3D results and classical 2D turbulence studies lies in the stability and lifetime of these coherent structures. In 2D turbulence, the inverse energy cascade stabilizes large vortices, allowing them to trap tracers for anomalously long durations, thereby generating the heavy-tailed waiting-time distributions required for Lévy flights. In contrast, in our 3D driven turbulence, the vortex tubes are highly dynamic and subject to rapid 3D instabilities, such as vortex stretching and the Crow instability. Consequently, while the vortices are indeed "sticky" and locally suppress chaotic separation, their finite lifetimes prevent tracers from remaining trapped long enough to produce macroscopic anomalous transport. The trapping events are inevitably truncated by the destruction or reconnection of the vortex structures, ensuring that the long-time macroscopic dispersion converges back to Gaussian statistics.

In conclusion, the Lagrangian dispersion in this large-scale solenoidal driven turbulence is highly isotropic and governed by Taylor's classical diffusion theory. The tracers exhibit a clear ballistic-to-diffusive crossover, with a well-defined Taylor diffusion coefficient. While coherent vortex structures locally trap tracers and suppress chaotic separation, these interactions are insufficiently persistent in 3D subsonic turbulence to induce macroscopic anomalous diffusion, resulting in strictly Gaussian displacement statistics over multiple eddy turnover times.
