The current analysis provides a robust characterization of Lagrangian transport in 3D solenoidal turbulence, successfully distinguishing between large-scale sweeping effects and small-scale vortex trapping. However, the interpretation of the results requires refinement to avoid over-attributing physical meaning to artifacts.

**Critical Weaknesses and Gaps:**
1. **Misinterpretation of "Subdiffusion":** You identify a late-time drop in $\alpha(t)$ to $\approx 0.89$ and label it as "subdiffusive." This is physically incorrect in the context of periodic boundary conditions. In a periodic box, the MSD must saturate at a value related to the box size ($L^2/6$). The "subdiffusive" slope is simply the mathematical consequence of the MSD approaching this saturation limit. You should explicitly state that the system is reaching the *geometric saturation limit* rather than a physical subdiffusive regime.
2. **Anisotropy Metric Sensitivity:** The anisotropy ratio $\lambda = MSD_{\parallel} / MSD_{\perp}$ is calculated relative to the *initial* large-scale velocity. Since the forcing is continuous and the flow is turbulent, the "local large-scale velocity" vector rotates and decorrelates over time. Using the initial vector as a fixed reference frame for the entire trajectory is a significant methodological flaw. The anisotropy should be re-evaluated using a *running* average of the large-scale velocity field or a frame that tracks the local forcing direction.
3. **FTLE Interpretation:** You interpret lower FTLE in vortex cores as "sticky" regions. While correct, you missed the opportunity to link this to the *Lagrangian Coherent Structures (LCS)*. The FTLE field is not just a proxy for chaos; it is the primary tool for identifying the manifolds that govern transport. You should explicitly map the "Free" tracers to the hyperbolic regions (ridges of the FTLE field) to provide a more rigorous structural explanation for the observed dispersion.

**Actionable Recommendations:**
1. **Correct the Scaling Analysis:** Remove the claim of "subdiffusion." Re-plot the MSD and explicitly show the theoretical saturation limit ($L^2/6$) for a periodic box. This will demonstrate that the "drop" in $\alpha$ is a predictable geometric effect, strengthening the paper's rigor.
2. **Dynamic Anisotropy:** Instead of a static reference vector, compute the anisotropy relative to the *instantaneous* large-scale velocity field (filtered at $n=1-3$). This will reveal whether the anisotropy is a persistent feature of the forcing or a transient effect that decays as the tracer loses memory of its initial injection state.
3. **Refine the Trapping Mechanism:** Since you have already computed the Q-criterion and FTLE, perform a correlation analysis between the *exit time* of tracers from high-Q regions and the *local FTLE value* at the point of exit. This will test if the "ejection" from vortex cores is driven by the local strain rate (high FTLE), providing a direct causal link between the vortex breakdown and the transport regime.
4. **Avoid Redundant Simulations:** Do not attempt to "fix" the finite-size effect by changing the domain. Instead, use the existing data to calculate the *Lagrangian Integral Time Scale* ($\tau_L$) via the VACF. If $\tau_L$ is significantly smaller than the time to reach the box boundary, you can argue that the early-time physics (the primary focus) is physically valid and independent of the box size.

The study is currently well-positioned to argue that 3D turbulence is fundamentally different from 2D due to the rapid destabilization of coherent structures. Focus the next iteration on the *transition* from vortex-dominated transport to strain-dominated transport. By quantifying the "residence time" in high-Q regions versus the "travel time" in high-FTLE regions, you can provide a quantitative model for how 3D turbulence "shuffles" tracers, which is more valuable than simply fitting power laws.
